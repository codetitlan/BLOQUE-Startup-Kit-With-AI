# 1-Hour Workshop: Using AI Agents to Build a Strong Early-Stage Pitch Deck

## Audience
Early-stage entrepreneurs in incubation, with mixed backgrounds and uneven familiarity with fundraising, storytelling, and AI tools.

## Workshop Goal
By the end of the session, participants should know how to use AI agents to:

- clarify their startup story
- structure a credible 10–12 slide pitch deck
- improve weak slides with better evidence and sharper claims
- avoid common AI failure modes such as vagueness, hallucinations, and generic decks

## Core Message
AI agents do not replace founder thinking. They accelerate it.

The founder still owns:

- the truth of the business
- the customer insight
- the strategic choices
- the final narrative

AI is most useful for:

- extracting a story from messy notes
- turning assumptions into explicit claims
- generating variants fast
- stress-testing the clarity of the deck

## Learning Objectives
At the end of the workshop, participants will be able to:

1. explain the difference between using AI as a writer and using AI as an agent
2. assemble the minimum inputs needed for a useful pitch-deck workflow
3. run a simple multi-step agent workflow from raw startup notes to slide outline
4. critique an AI-generated deck and improve it using better prompts and better evidence
5. leave with a repeatable prompt sequence they can reuse after the session

## Recommended 60-Minute Agenda

### 0:00–0:05 — Opening
- Introduce the goal: build a solid early-stage deck faster without outsourcing judgment
- Set expectation: this is about workflow, not about one specific AI tool
- Show the target output: a clean 10–12 slide deck outline

### 0:05–0:12 — What Makes a Good Early-Stage Deck
- Explain the minimum investor questions:
  - what problem exists?
  - why is it painful and urgent?
  - why is this solution compelling?
  - why this team?
  - what proof exists so far?
  - what happens next?
- Use this baseline deck structure:
  1. Title / one-line thesis
  2. Problem
  3. Customer / market context
  4. Solution
  5. Product demo / how it works
  6. Business model
  7. Traction / validation
  8. Competition / alternatives
  9. Go-to-market
  10. Team
  11. Roadmap / milestones
  12. Ask

### 0:12–0:20 — What AI Agents Are Good At
- Distinguish three modes:
  - chatbot mode: answers one request at a time
  - copilot mode: helps edit a slide or phrase
  - agent mode: follows a role, uses context, completes a multi-step task
- Explain the practical agent roles for this workshop:
  - research assistant
  - narrative strategist
  - skeptical investor
  - slide editor
- Teach one rule: the quality of the output depends on the quality of the founder inputs

### 0:20–0:35 — Live Workflow Demo
- Show a simple sequence:
  1. give the agent startup context
  2. ask it to extract the key facts, assumptions, and gaps
  3. ask it to propose a pitch narrative
  4. ask it to convert that narrative into a 10–12 slide outline
  5. ask it to critique the outline like an investor
  6. revise the outline
- During the demo, point out where the founder must intervene:
  - to correct false assumptions
  - to remove empty claims
  - to replace adjectives with evidence
  - to keep the story specific

### 0:35–0:48 — Participant Exercise
- Ask each participant to paste a rough startup description into the workflow
- Minimum input:
  - startup name
  - customer
  - problem
  - current solution
  - why now
  - business model guess
  - evidence so far
  - key unknowns
- Task:
  - generate a draft deck outline
  - identify the three weakest slides
  - improve them using evidence or sharper wording

### 0:48–0:55 — Debrief
- Ask participants:
  - which slides came easily?
  - which slides stayed vague?
  - where did AI overstate certainty?
  - what evidence is missing from the story?
- Reinforce the operating principle:
  - weak inputs produce generic decks
  - better facts produce better investor narratives

### 0:55–1:00 — Close
- Summarize the workflow
- Give them the prompt pack
- Assign follow-up:
  - finish a first draft deck this week
  - test it with one mentor or peer
  - revise based on objections, not aesthetics alone

## Facilitator Notes

### Keep the workshop practical
- Avoid long theory on fundraising
- Keep examples concrete and simple
- Translate jargon for non-technical founders
- Avoid tool tribalism; the method matters more than the brand

### Normalize incomplete inputs
Many incubation-stage founders do not yet have:

- revenue
- a finished product
- precise TAM figures
- a finalized business model

That is acceptable. The deck should still be honest and decision-useful. Replace weak certainty with:

- early signals
- customer discovery evidence
- experiments run
- hypotheses to validate

### Teach good AI hygiene
- never let the model invent traction
- never let the model invent customers or partnerships
- challenge market-size claims
- prefer plain language over buzzwords
- ask for concise outputs with explicit assumptions

## Suggested Demo Startup
Use one fictional but realistic example so the group can focus on method rather than domain expertise.

Example:

- startup: `RutaFlow`
- customer: small urban logistics companies in Latin America
- problem: dispatch is still coordinated across WhatsApp, spreadsheets, and phone calls
- solution: lightweight operations software for route assignment, live status, and delivery incidents
- why now: smaller operators are digitizing, but enterprise tools are too expensive and too complex
- business model: monthly SaaS subscription priced by active vehicles
- traction: 18 pilot customers, 6 weekly active, founders completed 40 interviews
- risk: retention and willingness to pay remain unproven

## Slide-by-Slide Guidance

### 1. Title
- company name
- one-line value proposition
- optional short proof point

### 2. Problem
- specific customer pain
- frequency and cost of the problem
- current workaround

### 3. Customer / Market Context
- who feels the pain most
- why this segment matters first
- why this problem matters now

### 4. Solution
- what the product does
- why it is meaningfully better than current alternatives

### 5. Product
- how it works in simple terms
- what the user sees or does

### 6. Business Model
- who pays
- how pricing works
- what behavior drives revenue

### 7. Traction / Validation
- pilots
- interviews
- usage
- waitlist
- conversion
- retention signals

### 8. Competition / Alternatives
- status quo
- direct competitors
- adjacent substitutes
- why the startup wins for the chosen segment

### 9. Go-to-Market
- first wedge
- customer acquisition motion
- who sells
- how trust is built

### 10. Team
- why this team can solve this problem
- relevant domain, technical, or execution credibility

### 11. Roadmap / Milestones
- next 6–12 months
- key de-risking milestones
- avoid feature laundry lists

### 12. Ask
- what the company is asking for now
- capital, pilots, intros, advisors, or hires
- what the support unlocks

## Common Failure Modes to Show During the Workshop
- problem slide uses generic pain statements
- solution slide sounds like a slogan
- traction slide confuses activity with proof
- competition slide pretends there are no alternatives
- TAM slide is huge but irrelevant
- team slide lists résumés instead of founder-market fit
- AI-generated copy sounds polished but empty

## Simple Teaching Framework
Use this rule for every slide:

`claim -> evidence -> implication`

Example:

- claim: dispatch is chaotic for small operators
- evidence: interviews show teams switching across WhatsApp, calls, and spreadsheets every day
- implication: there is room for a simpler operations layer designed for smaller fleets

## What Participants Should Leave With
- one draft deck outline
- one revised version after critique
- one list of missing evidence
- one repeatable prompt workflow

## Delivery Tips
- If the room is mixed in experience, ask advanced founders to improve their weakest slide instead of generating a first outline
- If time runs short, skip design discussion and stay focused on narrative quality
- If participants are blocked, ask them to answer:
  - who has the pain?
  - what do they do today?
  - why is that broken?
  - what proof do you have that this matters?

## Optional Homework
- turn the outline into actual slides
- interview 3 potential customers and update the problem slide
- rewrite the traction slide with only verifiable evidence
- ask an AI agent to act as a skeptical investor and produce 10 objections
